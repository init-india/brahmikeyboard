Getting started

  • Open the app, tap Enable in system settings, switch the keyboard on, then tap Choose keyboard and pick Brahmi Keyboard.
  • Words are sent when you press Space. Until then the word waits in the preview strip above the keys.
  • In password fields the preview and the field show asterisks, but the app receives the real text. The keyboard has no network access and sends nothing anywhere.

The Mode key

  • The Mode key has a small label under it: cus (custom mode), br (Brahmi mode) or pb (Pure Brahmi mode). Tap it to go cus, then br, then pb, then back to cus. Your last mode is remembered.
  • Hold the Mode key for quick English: English in, English out, label eng. Hold it again (or tap it) to go back to the mode you were in.
  • The button next to the space bar shows what is converted into what, for example Kan→Brh in Brahmi mode, Brh→Kan in Pure Brahmi mode and Eng→Kan in custom mode. Tap it to choose languages.
  • The Mode key, the joiner key and the Go key have an amber border because they are the keys you will use most.

Custom mode (cus)

  • Turns what you type into the script of any of the 21 languages, or leaves it as English.
  • Type with English: type phonetically, for example namaste gives ನಮಸ್ತೇ with Kannada output. The small letters under each key show what it gives in the output script. Capital letters have special meanings: T, D, N, L are the retroflex letters, M is the anusvara, H is the visarga, and Sh is ष.
  • Type with a language's own keys: the keys of the Pure Brahmi layout appear with that language's letters, and the small letter under each key is the output script. Hindi keys with Tamil output turn नोकेश्वर into நோகேஶ்வர.
  • English to English leaves the text exactly as typed.
  • Choose what you type with, and the output script, in Settings or with the language button. Custom mode has no Ashokan restrictions.
  • When you type with English and the output is another script, a row of vowel signs and the joiner appears, so you can type words like नोकेश्वर: n, ◌ो, k, ◌े, sh, joiner, v, a, r.

Brahmi mode (br)

  • Converts your language into Ashokan Brahmi. The keys show your preferred language's letters, with the Brahmi letter in small type under each key. You type your language and the app receives Brahmi.
  • The preview shows your language's letters, then an arrow, then the Brahmi.
  • For a conjunct, type consonant, joiner, consonant (the joiner is in the second row, in the middle).
  • Number keys show your language's digits with the Ashokan numeral under them. Type 2, 5, 6 to get 256 written the Ashokan way.

Pure Brahmi mode (pb)

  • The keys are Brahmi letters, with your language's letter in small type. The preview shows how it reads in your preferred language.
  • Type a consonant, then a vowel sign from the second row. Example: 𑀓 then ◌𑀸 gives 𑀓𑀸.
  • For a conjunct, type consonant, joiner, consonant.
  • The shift key does nothing in this layout.

Keys

  • Space sends the word and a space. ↵ sends the word and starts a new line.
  • Go (far right of the bottom row) sends the word waiting in the preview to the app, with no space or new line, and the keyboard stays open. When nothing is waiting in the preview, Go runs the app's own action (go, search or send). Whether an app accepts it is up to the app: for a messaging app, type your message, press Go to move it into the message box, then press Go again to send. Some apps ignore it.
  • ⌫ removes one character; hold it to keep deleting. Del clears the whole word, or removes the last word when nothing is waiting.
  • 123 shows numbers and symbols; ABC or ← back returns. The small smiley on the 123 key is a reminder that the emoji picker is there: press 123, then the 😊 key.

Emoji

  • The emoji picker works in every mode. Pick a group with the tabs at the top (the clock tab holds the ones you used recently), scroll, and tap an emoji. A word waiting in the preview is sent first, then the emoji. Use ABC to return.
  • Only emoji your phone can draw are shown, so newer phones show more.

Size, position and colours (Settings)

  • Key height, Space under the keys (lifts the keyboard) and Keyboard width with a left, centre or right position make the keyboard easier to use one-handed. If the bottom row sits on the phone's back and home buttons, raise the space under the keys, or leave the automatic option on.
  • Keyboard colour, Preview strip colour, Preview letter colour, Key colour and Letter colour: tap a colour or type a code like #1E3A5F, then Apply. Theme default goes back to the theme. The theme can be system, light or dark.
  • Size and colour changes show the next time the keyboard opens.

Numbers

  • Ashokan numerals are not positional and have no zero. 1 to 9 have their own signs, as do 10, 20 to 90, 100 and 1000.
  • Hundreds and thousands join a digit to the 100 or 1000 sign. 256 is written as 2 hundreds, 50 and 6.
  • In Brahmi and Pure Brahmi mode, typed numbers are converted to Ashokan numerals, from 1 to 9999. A number above 9999, or a lone 0, is refused with a red message. Custom mode has no limit.

What cannot be typed in Brahmi mode

  • Visarga, candrabindu, nukta, accent marks, avagraha, and the candra and short vowels: not used in Ashokan Brahmi.
  • Vocalic ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ and their signs: later additions.
  • Conjuncts that include ra, such as kra, tra, rka and rma.
  • Zero.
  • Letters found only in one modern language, such as Tamil ழ, ற and ன, are not on the Brahmi keys yet.
  • A red message appears when a key is refused. The key is ignored and the rest of the word continues.

Orange warnings

  • An orange message means the letter is typed normally but is not found in Ashokan script, so it is post-Ashokan.
  • श and ष get a post-Ashokan warning. For ऐ and औ, and the signs ै and ौ (खै, गौ), the message says Ashokan script treats them as ए and ओ. You still get the actual ai and au letters in both Brahmi and Pure Brahmi mode.

About Ashokan Brahmi

  • An abugida: each consonant carries a short a, other vowels are written with signs, and independent vowel letters start a word. It is written left to right.
  • The best known early dated examples are the edicts of Ashoka, about 250 to 232 BCE, which give the reference forms.
  • Vowels: a, ā, i, ī, u, ū, e, ai, o, and the sign for au.
  • 34 consonants: five groups of five, then y r l v, three sibilants, h and ḷ.
  • Conjuncts are written by stacking letters downward. This keyboard uses the Unicode joiner sign and the font draws the stack.
  • The anusvara is used. Ancient sources list 11 or 12 vowels at this stage, adding aṃ or aḥ.
  • Text is stored in the Unicode Brahmi block (U+11000 to U+1107F). It pastes anywhere, but the reader needs a Brahmi font, and letter shapes depend on that font.

Limits of this version

  • The Ashokan rules come from reference sources, not from a check of every inscription.
  • The tables for several languages have not been checked by native speakers.
  • Urdu, Kashmiri, Sindhi, Santali and Manipuri previews are phonetic approximations, not dictionary spellings.
